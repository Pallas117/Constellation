01_GAUSS · STORM STORY

The night the sky went red.

Observed · NOAA SWPC

10–11 May 2024. The top level of NOAA’s geomagnetic scale, held for six hours. This is the record, hour by hour. Then a question: what were a model’s answers worth that night?

01_THE_RECORD

Eighteen hours.

  1. 10 May · 12:00 UT

    12:00

    The record opens.

    Six hours before the first severe reading. Nothing below is simulated: every level is the observed one.

  2. 10 May · 18:00–21:00 UT

    G4

    Severe.

    Kp 8.67 for the three-hour interval. One step below the top of the scale.

  3. 10 May 21:00 – 11 May 03:00 UT

    G5

    Extreme.

    Kp 9, two intervals in a row. There is no level above this one.

  4. 11 May · 01:17 UT

    X5.8

    And then the flare.

    With G5 still in force, a radio blackout begins: R3 from 01:17. Six minutes later the X-ray peak arrives, X5.8 at 01:23.

Source: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center (planetary Kp, the G and R scales, GOES X-ray flux), from the archive the Gauss replay runs on, 10 May 12:00 UT to 11 May 06:00 UT. Kp is measured in three-hour intervals, so each G level is dated by its interval.

02_IN_ORBIT

Now put a model in orbit that night.

Illustration · not a mission record

It keeps answering through all of it. Afterwards, one question matters: which answers do you trust?

A per-record evidence pack would have shown

  • The conditions each answer was made under: G4 from 18:00, G5 from 21:00, R3 from 01:17
  • How old its inputs were when it ran, and which were missing
  • Its state: Nominal Degraded Abstain Unknown Blocked
  • The reason, in plain words, on the record itself

What it would not say

  • Anything about a real spacecraft or operator. This page describes none
  • What went wrong. It is documented risk: which answers were made under which conditions
  • Hardware figures. Without evidence for the processor, none are given
  • What to do next. Gauss is advisory: decisions stay with the operators
